Households use their own experiences to form expectations about the macroeconomy. Changes in household income can thus affect decisions through both resources and information. I study the importance of this channel in a heterogeneous-agent model in which households infer aggregate movements from their own cash flows. When macroeconomic conditions are not fully observed, countercyclical transfers are not only resource windfalls. In addition, they signal to households that aggregate conditions are weak. Evidence from a randomized information experiment supports this mechanism: fiscal information lowers GDP forecasts and reduces durable spending intentions, with the decline in the latter strongest among high-income households. To solve the model, I develop a global method that combines Bayesian filtering with approximate aggregation. On impact, the information channel reduces the consumption response to a transfer from about 38 cents per dollar under full information to about 7 cents under dispersed information. As in the data, negative spending responses to information are strongest at the top of the distribution. Moreover, when households infer aggregate conditions from cash flows, they learn about aggregate shocks gradually, generating hump-shaped consumption responses. I find that targeting transfers toward low-income households can mitigate the effects of the information channel, both raising the impact consumption response and restoring a front-loaded aggregate response.
